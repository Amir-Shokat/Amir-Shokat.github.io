The question

Media bias is a hot topic, but claims about it are often based on a few examples. This project measures it: take every article in a sample of 380 CNBC news stories from 2006 to 2021, measure the tone of each one, and compare the 52 articles that mention Trump with the rest.

How tone is measured

The tone score works like a dictionary: each word has a value from very negative (−1) to very positive (+1). "Excellent" scores high, "terrible" low, and most words are neutral. Words like "very" strengthen the next word, and "not" flips it. The article's tone is the average. Try it on any sentence:

A JavaScript port of TextBlob, the tool used in the study, with the same word list and rules for "very", "not" and "!". On 300 test sentences from the articles it gives exactly the same score as TextBlob 98% of the time.

Its limits: a general dictionary doesn't understand finance or politics. "Shares crashed" and "a tariff war" are obviously bad news to a reader, but many such words are neutral in the dictionary. That's why the study compares groups of articles scored the same way, instead of reading much into one score.

Results

Each dot is one article. Orange dots mention Trump. Most articles sit slightly above zero: business news is mildly positive on average.

  • Tone: the two groups are practically identical, also when only articles from June 2015 onwards (the start of his campaign) are compared.
  • Subjectivity: articles that mention Trump use slightly less opinionated language, and this difference is statistically significant. These are mostly political and policy reports, which are written more factually than market commentary.
  • Sentences about Trump himself are close to neutral on average ().

What I learned

  • Two averages aren't a finding. The original assignment compared two numbers by eye. Adding significance tests and confidence intervals is what turns it into evidence.
  • Pick measures that can disagree with you. The original claimed the Trump articles were "very similar" based on a similarity score that is high for almost any two long texts. A better measure (TF-IDF) showed they're not.
  • Be clear about what a tool can't do. Dictionary sentiment is a blunt instrument; next steps would be comparing several politicians and using a finance-trained language model.
