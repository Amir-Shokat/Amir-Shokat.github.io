The question

When tensions rise between countries, investors traditionally buy gold: it can't be printed and holds its value. Bitcoin also can't be printed, which led to the slogan "digital gold". If that's true, Bitcoin should rise when geopolitical risk rises.

To measure geopolitical risk, I used the Geopolitical Risk (GPR) index by economists at the US Federal Reserve. It counts newspaper articles about wars, terrorism and tensions between countries every day.

Big jumps in risk (the Paris attacks, the invasion of Ukraine, the war in Gaza) don't line up with clear moves in either asset. To go beyond eyeballing, the study runs a regression on every trading day, controlling for interest rates and credit conditions, which also move both assets.

What the data says

How returns react to a 10% jump in geopolitical risk

Explore

  • Same day: neither asset moves in a statistically meaningful way when risk jumps.
  • Gold, one day later: gold rises by about the day after a 10% jump in risk, and that effect is statistically significant. It looks like a slow-moving safe haven.
  • Bitcoin: its estimates are mostly negative. In 2019-2024 it tended to fall when risk rose, the opposite of a hedge.

The biggest scares

Averages can hide what happens in extreme moments, so I also looked only at the 10% of days with the largest jumps in geopolitical risk.

Error bars show ±2 standard errors. Bitcoin's average on scare days looks higher, but it is far too noisy to be distinguished from normal days, and its volatility rises on those days.

What I learned

  • Slogans need testing. "Digital gold" doesn't survive contact with the data, at least as a geopolitical hedge.
  • Use the right standard errors. Volatility measured over rolling windows is strongly autocorrelated. Ordinary p-values overstate significance; robust (HAC) errors fix that.
  • Make research reproducible. The original group project left tables but no code. I rebuilt it so a single command downloads the data and reproduces every number.
