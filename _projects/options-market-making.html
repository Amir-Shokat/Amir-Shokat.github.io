The idea in plain words

A market maker is like a currency exchange booth at an airport: it always shows a price to buy and a slightly higher price to sell. It doesn't try to guess where prices go next. It earns the small difference between the two prices, many times a day.

In this course project, five of us built a market maker for options, contracts that give the right to buy (a call) or sell (a put) a stock at a fixed price later. The bot ran live on Optiver's training exchange against other teams' bots, and we finished second.

Three things had to work at the same time:

  1. Price each option fairly, every few seconds, as the stock moves.
  2. Quote buy and sell prices around that fair value without being picked off.
  3. Hedge: trade the stock to cancel the risk that the options pick up.

Step 1: what is an option worth?

The bot uses the Black-Scholes formula, the classic model for pricing options. Type in any numbers to see how the value reacts. The thin line shows what the option pays at expiry, and the thick line shows what it's worth today, while there's still time for the stock to move.

Delta: how much the option moves when the stock moves by 1. Gamma: how fast delta itself changes. Vega: how much value is added if volatility rises by one percentage point. Theta: value lost per day as time passes.

Step 2: quoting without getting hurt

Knowing the fair value isn't enough. The bot needs a bid (the price it buys at) and an ask (the price it sells at). The rules we settled on:

  • Improve the best price in the market by one tick (0.10) to be first in line, but never cross fair value. Buying above what something is worth loses money on every trade.
  • If the bot already owns too many options (above 60 of the limit of 100), it stops competing to buy and makes its sell price attractive, so the extra inventory is sold off. The opposite applies when it owes too many.
  • Trade more when the market's prices are far from fair value: 30 options per tick of edge, up to 100.

Step 3: hedging away the risk

Every option the bot trades makes it slightly exposed to the stock price. The exposure is measured by delta: holding 10 calls with a delta of 0.6 is like holding 6 shares. After each round of quotes, the bot adds up the delta of all its options and buys or sells shares to bring the total back to zero.

When the main stock listing hit its trading limit, the bot switched to a second, less liquid listing of the same stock, which doubled its hedging capacity.

What I learned

  • The model is only as good as its inputs. In the simulation, if the bot prices with the wrong volatility, its quotes sit on the wrong side of the market and the profit disappears.
  • Small rules matter. When I rebuilt the code I found a quote that could be placed above fair value, a guaranteed loss, and a size calculation that read the same price twice. Both are fixed and covered by tests now.
  • Risk control is the product. Earning the spread is easy; earning it without building up a big one-sided position is the hard part.
