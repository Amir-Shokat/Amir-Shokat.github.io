The idea in plain words

The yield curve shows the interest rate for borrowing over 1 year, 2 years, and so on up to 30 years. Banks and pension funds hold positions all along it, so they need to know: if rates move tomorrow, how much could we lose?

Tracking 15 rates separately is hard. But they don't move independently: most days the whole curve shifts up or down together. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the few typical movements that explain almost everything.

The bad-data story

In the original assignment, the third movement looked odd: a sharp dip only at the 7, 8 and 9-year rates. While rebuilding the project I traced it to a single day: on 25 May 2015, a market holiday, the data feed showed those three rates dropping by up to 0.84 percentage points, only to snap back the next morning. One bad day out of 4,682 was enough to change the result.

Third movement, before and after removing one bad day

EUR swap rates, 1999 to 2017 (the course data). After cleaning, the third movement has the textbook "bow" shape: short and long ends move one way, the middle the other.

The rebuilt tool finds such quotes automatically: a big move in one maturity that its neighbours don't share, reversed the next day. It deliberately leaves alone big moves that the whole curve shares, such as October 2008 or March 2023, because those were real.

What I learned

  • Check the data before trusting the model. The most interesting result in the original work was a data error.
  • Simple models can understate risk. For the textbook portfolio, using two movements instead of three gives a loss estimate about 20% too low, and real data has fatter tails than the normal distribution assumes.
  • Make it reproducible. The original used licensed data; the rebuild downloads free ECB data, so anyone can check the numbers.
