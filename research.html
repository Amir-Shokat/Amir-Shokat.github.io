Research

Climate risk in asset prices

I want to understand how climate risk reaches stock prices: where the damage happens, who prices it, and what happens when those two are not the same place.

MSc thesis

Warming Trends: Analyzing the Impact of Temperature Anomalies on the Risk and Return of the US Stock Market

I grew up in Birjand, a dry city in eastern Iran where every summer is hotter than the last. When the heat peaks, the power goes out and work stops, so for me heat was always an economic problem. My thesis asked whether the stock market sees it too: I matched about 18,000 US firms to the local temperature at their headquarters, month by month from 1995 to 2024, and tested whether unusual heat or cold moves their returns or volatility.

Utrecht University, June 2024. Supervisors: Dr. Thomas Walther and Prof. dr. Frank Verbeeten.

June 2024 · original

The thesis as submitted

This is the original version I handed in. It found no effect of temperature on returns, and some weak evidence, mostly significant only at the 10% level, that unusual temperatures are linked to volatility.

2026 · revised

The rebuilt and corrected version

I want to keep working on this question, so after graduating I rebuilt the study from scratch. The rebuild found errors in the original data (for example, many firms were given another town's weather), so the calculations changed. With the data corrected, there is no detectable effect on returns or volatility. This is the version I build on.

Future research

The thesis looked for heat at a firm's headquarters and found nothing. But most of a firm's workers are in plants and stores in other states, and the prices are set by shareholders who live somewhere else again and feel their own heat. So the real question is where to look.

01

Heat where the firm operates

Heat at a firm's plants slows work and raises costs. If that is what matters, earnings should fall, and the stock price should drop when they are announced and stay down.

02

Heat where the shareholders live

Heat in investors' own cities changes nothing at the firm, but it can change what investors believe about climate risk. If that is what moves prices, they should move with no change in earnings, and then reverse.

03

Both, for the same firms

As far as I know, no one has put the two side by side. Plant locations come from 10-K filings and shareholders from 13F filings. In a pilot of 50 firms from my thesis sample, more than half of those that report their sites have most of them outside their headquarters state.

04

Beyond US heat

If prices follow where investors live and not where the damage is, markets misjudge which firms are exposed. I would start with heat in the US and then take the same design to floods and to European markets.

This is the research I hope to continue in a PhD. In the long run I would like work like this to help central banks measure climate risk, and in time help economies like Iran's, where it is needed most.

Related work: Pankratz and Schiller (2024) show that heat at suppliers' sites cuts operating income along the supply chain; Alekseev, Giglio, Maingi, Selgrad and Stroebel (2022) show that fund managers trade differently after extreme heat in their own city.
