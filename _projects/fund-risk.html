What a fund risk report answers

As a risk analyst I prepared regular risk reports for the bank's mutual funds. Each one answers a few simple questions for the fund managers and the compliance team:

  • How much does the fund swing? Volatility, and the worst fall from a peak.
  • Is the return worth the risk? Sharpe and Sortino ratios compare return with risk taken.
  • How bad can a normal bad day get? Value at Risk: the loss that should be exceeded on only 1 day in 100.
  • Can we sell if we need to? How many days it would take to sell a holding without moving its price.
  • What if interest rates rise? The duration of the bond holdings.

The real reports used client data, which stays confidential. The demos below run the same calculations on made-up funds, or on numbers you paste in yourself.

Errors I fixed

Rebuilding the notebooks as a tested package surfaced real mistakes, some of which had been copied into many reports:

  • Dates off by about 80 days. Persian (Jalali) dates were converted to Western dates by adding 621 to the year. The Persian year starts on 20 or 21 March, so every bond maturity was shifted, and some dates were impossible. The toolkit now converts exactly, with tests that pin known dates.
  • Bond durations. Coupons were discounted at the coupon rate instead of the market yield, and the final repayment was timed differently from the last coupon. Durations are now computed from a proper payment schedule and checked against numerical derivatives.
  • The Sortino ratio measured how spread out losses were rather than how large, which can make a riskier fund look safer.
  • Reconciliation. Bank account numbers copied out of right-to-left documents came back reversed (123-456 became 456-123). The fix sorted the pieces, which could merge two different accounts; the new key only treats a reversed order as the same account.

What I learned

  • Copy-paste is a risk. When the same calculation lives in 60 notebooks, a bug lives in 60 places. One tested function fixes it everywhere.
  • Backtest your risk model. A VaR number means little until you check how often it was actually exceeded. The toolkit does this automatically (the Kupiec test).
  • Local details matter. The Persian calendar, mixed Arabic and Persian characters and right-to-left text caused more real-world errors than any formula.
